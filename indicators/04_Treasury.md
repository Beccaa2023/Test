# Treasury Handbook

## Purpose

The Treasury Handbook explains how MOS interprets the U.S. Treasury market and its role in macroeconomic analysis.

Treasury securities are the benchmark risk-free assets of the global financial system.

Their yields reflect expectations for economic growth, inflation, monetary policy and risk appetite.

MOS therefore treats the Treasury market as the primary market-based indicator of macroeconomic expectations.

---

Treasury yields are determined by five major forces.

Economic Growth

# Core Indicator 1

## 2-Year Treasury Yield

Importance

★★★★★

Classification

Market-Based

---

### Why It Matters

The 2-year Treasury yield is highly sensitive to expectations for Federal Reserve policy.

It often moves before the policy rate itself changes.

MOS treats the 2-year yield as the market's view of future short-term interest rates.

---

### Interpretation

2-Year Yield Rising

↓

Markets expect tighter monetary policy.

2-Year Yield Falling

↓

Markets expect easier monetary policy.

---

# Core Indicator 2

## 10-Year Treasury Yield

Importance

★★★★★

Classification

Market-Based

---

### Why It Matters

The 10-year Treasury yield reflects long-term expectations for growth, inflation and real interest rates.

It serves as the benchmark discount rate for many financial assets.

Changes in the 10-year yield affect mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs and equity valuations.

---

### Interpretation

10-Year Yield Rising

May indicate:

• Stronger growth expectations

• Higher inflation expectations

• Higher real yields

• Increased term premium

MOS identifies which factor is dominant before drawing conclusions.

# Core Indicator 3

## Yield Curve

Importance

★★★★★

Classification

Leading

---

### What is the Yield Curve?

The yield curve plots Treasury yields across different maturities.

The shape of the curve provides information about market expectations.

---

### Common Shapes

Normal

Long-term yields exceed short-term yields.

Often associated with economic expansion.

Flat

Economic uncertainty increases.

Markets expect slower growth.

Inverted

Short-term yields exceed long-term yields.

Historically associated with elevated recession risk.

Steepening

Can result from stronger growth expectations or policy easing.

MOS distinguishes between bullish and bearish steepening.

---

### Key Spreads

10Y – 2Y

10Y – 3M

5Y – 2Y

These spreads are monitored continuously.

# Core Indicator 4

## Real Yield (TIPS)

Importance

★★★★★

Classification

Market-Based

---

### Why Real Yield Matters

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) provide an estimate of inflation-adjusted interest rates.

Real yields measure the true cost of capital after accounting for expected inflation.

MOS considers real yields one of the most important drivers of long-duration asset prices.

---

### Interpretation

Real Yield Rising

↓

Financial conditions tighten.

↓

Valuation pressure increases.

↓

Gold and growth equities often face headwinds.

Real Yield Falling

↓

Financial conditions ease.

↓

Supportive for long-duration assets.

# Core Indicator 6

## Term Premium

Importance

★★★★☆

---

### What is Term Premium?

Term premium is the additional compensation investors require for holding long-term bonds instead of rolling over short-term securities.

It reflects uncertainty rather than expected policy rates.

Changes in term premium may significantly affect long-term yields even when growth and inflation expectations remain stable.

# Common Analytical Mistakes

Mistake 1

Assuming all yield increases are bearish.

Correction:

Identify whether the move is driven by growth, inflation, real yields or term premium.

---

Mistake 2

Ignoring real yields.

Correction:

Separate nominal yields into real yields and inflation expectations.

---

Mistake 3

Treating yield curve inversion as an immediate recession signal.

Correction:

An inverted curve indicates elevated recession probability, not precise timing.

---

Mistake 4

Ignoring Treasury auctions.

Correction:

Auction demand can affect market pricing, especially during periods of heavy debt issuance.

# Key Questions

Every Treasury analysis should answer:

Which factor is driving yields?

What is the market pricing for future Federal Reserve policy?

Are real yields tightening or easing financial conditions?

What does the yield curve imply about the business cycle?

How should Treasury market signals influence portfolio positioning?

# Final Principle

Treasury yields are not merely interest rates.

They are the market's continuously updated assessment of growth, inflation, monetary policy and risk.

MOS uses the Treasury market as the central transmission mechanism linking the real economy to financial assets.
