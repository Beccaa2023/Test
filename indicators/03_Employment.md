# Employment Handbook

## Purpose

The Employment Handbook explains how MOS evaluates labor market conditions and interprets their implications for economic growth, inflation and monetary policy.

Employment is the primary transmission channel between businesses and households.

A strong labor market supports income, consumption and corporate earnings.

A weakening labor market often signals the late stage of the business cycle.

MOS therefore treats employment as one of the most important macro pillars.

Coincident

---

### What is Payroll?

Nonfarm Payrolls measure the monthly change in employed workers excluding farm workers, private household employees and several small categories.

Payrolls provide the most widely followed measure of labor market expansion.

---

### Market Interpretation

Payrolls above expectations

↓

Growth stronger

↓

Higher inflation risk

↓

Higher Treasury yields

↓

Higher probability of Fed tightening

Payrolls below expectations

↓

Growth slowing

↓

Lower yields

↓

Higher probability of Fed easing

---

### MOS Interpretation

Never evaluate Payroll alone.

Always examine

Headline

Revision

Three-month average

Sector composition

Government employment

Private employment

Average weekly hours

---

### Common Mistakes

Strong payroll growth does not always imply economic acceleration.

Temporary hiring or public-sector hiring may distort the headline.

MOS focuses on sustainable private-sector employment growth.

---

### Why JOLTS Matters

JOLTS measures labor demand.

Firms usually reduce vacancies before reducing headcount.

Therefore job openings often decline before unemployment rises.

MOS considers JOLTS one of the best early indicators of labor market turning points.

---

### Key Metrics

Job Openings

Hires

Quits

Layoffs

Vacancy Rate

The quits rate reflects worker confidence.

High quits usually indicate a strong labor market.

### Why Claims Matter

Initial Claims measure new applications for unemployment benefits.

Because they are published weekly, they provide one of the earliest signals of labor market deterioration.

MOS monitors both the level and trend.

---

### Interpretation

Claims steadily rising

↓

Hiring slowing

↓

Employment weakening

↓

Consumption risk increasing

# Core Indicator 4

## Unemployment Rate

Importance

★★★★☆

Classification

Lagging

---

### Why It Matters

The unemployment rate measures the percentage of the labor force actively seeking work but unable to find employment.

It is a widely followed statistic but often reacts after economic conditions have already changed.

MOS therefore treats unemployment as confirmation rather than prediction.

---

### Sahm Rule

MOS monitors the Sahm Rule.

If the three-month average unemployment rate rises by 0.5 percentage points or more above its recent low,

historically this has often coincided with the onset of recession.

The Sahm Rule is used as a warning signal rather than a deterministic rule.

---

# Core Indicator 5

## Labor Force Participation Rate

Importance

★★★★☆

Classification

Coincident

---

Participation measures the share of the working-age population that is either employed or actively seeking work.

A declining unemployment rate caused by falling participation is less positive than one caused by stronger hiring.

MOS always analyses participation alongside unemployment.

---

# Core Indicator 6

## Prime-Age Employment-to-Population Ratio (Prime-age EPOP)

Importance

★★★★★

Classification

Coincident

---

Prime-age EPOP measures the employment rate of people aged 25–54.

Because it excludes most students and retirees, it provides a cleaner measure of labor market strength.

MOS considers Prime-age EPOP one of the highest-quality employment indicators.

---

## Average Hourly Earnings (AHE)

Importance

★★★★★

Classification

Coincident

---

### Why It Matters

Average Hourly Earnings measure nominal wage growth.

Persistent wage growth above productivity growth may contribute to services inflation.

MOS analyses wages together with productivity rather than in isolation.

# Core Indicator 8

## Average Weekly Hours

Importance

★★★★☆

Classification

Leading

---

Employers often reduce working hours before reducing headcount.

Falling weekly hours may therefore signal weakening labor demand before layoffs increase.

MOS treats this as an important leading indicator.

# Cross Confirmation

MOS requires multiple indicators to confirm labor conditions.

Example:

Payroll ↑

JOLTS ↑

Claims ↓

Prime-age EPOP ↑

↓

Example:

Payroll ↑

JOLTS ↓

Weekly Hours ↓

Claims ↑

↓

but leading indicators suggest weakening labor demand.

# Common Analytical Mistakes

Mistake 1

Focusing only on Payroll.

Correction:

Review JOLTS, Claims and Weekly Hours.

---

Mistake 2

Ignoring revisions.

Correction:

Payroll revisions often materially change the interpretation.

---

Mistake 3

Using unemployment as an early indicator.

Correction:

Monitor hiring and claims first.

---

Mistake 4

Ignoring labor force participation.

Correction:

Lower unemployment caused by shrinking labor supply is not necessarily a sign of strength.

# Key Questions

Every employment analysis should answer:

Is labor demand strengthening or weakening?

Are firms hiring, freezing hiring or laying off workers?

Are wages growing faster than productivity?

Are leading indicators confirming the headline payroll data?

What are the implications for consumption, inflation and Federal Reserve policy?

---

# Final Principle

Employment is not one statistic.

It is a dynamic system linking firms, households and the broader economy.

MOS seeks to identify changes in labor demand before they appear in unemployment statistics.
